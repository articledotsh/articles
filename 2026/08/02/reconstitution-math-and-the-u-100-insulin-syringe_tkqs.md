Anyone who has held a fresh vial of lyophilised peptide next to a U-100 insulin syringe has had the same moment: the vial is labelled in milligrams, the syringe is labelled in units, and nothing on either object explains how one becomes the other. It is a small gap, and it is where nearly all of the avoidable errors live.

The bridge is a single number, the concentration. You choose it when you decide how much bacteriostatic water to add.

concentration in mg per mL = vial strength in mg divided by water added in mL

A 10 mg vial with 2 mL of water is 5 mg/mL. The same vial with 1 mL is 10 mg/mL. Nothing about the peptide changed. Only the readability of your doses changed.

To get from a dose in milligrams to a number you can actually see on a syringe barrel, two steps:

volume in mL = dose in mg divided by concentration in mg per mL

units = volume in mL times 100

A 0.5 mg dose from a 5 mg/mL vial is 0.1 mL, which is 10 units. That is it. The whole calculation.

The practical advice hidden in this is about choosing the diluent volume deliberately rather than defaulting to whatever is on hand. Pick a volume that puts your usual dose somewhere between about 10 and 40 units. Under 5 units, the graduation marks are coarse relative to the dose and small drawing errors become large percentage errors. Over 50 units you are pushing more liquid than necessary and burning through the vial faster than the schedule assumes.

One more thing worth writing down: the concentration itself. A dose log that records only "10 units" is unreadable six weeks later when a new vial was mixed differently. Record the concentration alongside every entry, plus the date the vial was reconstituted, and the log stays interpretable no matter how the mixing changes.

Terminology is the other friction point in this area, and most of it is ordinary once someone defines it plainly. A short [glossary of the common terms](https://glp1.app/glossary) saves a lot of piecemeal searching.

None of the above is medical advice. Choosing a dose is a clinical question. Converting that dose into a mark on a barrel is only arithmetic, and arithmetic should not be the part that goes wrong.
